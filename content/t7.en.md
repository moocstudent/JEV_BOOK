## Three words that get spoken interchangeably

This is the book's technical core and the chapter most people skip. A System One model does not sell classification — a fine-tuned ModernBERT classifies too — it sells classification **with a calibrated probability**. To understand that, separate three words.

- **Accuracy**: the share of answers that were right, a number about a batch.
- **Confidence**: what the model reports about one input, a number about that row.
- **Calibration**: whether the two agree — among everything it called 0.9, were about nine in ten right?

@fig t7-reliab

A 70%-accurate but perfectly calibrated model beats an 85%-accurate one that always says 0.99. The first can be routed by certainty; the second can only be trusted wholesale, and trusting an 85% model wholesale lets one error in seven through.

## Three things computed live

- **Reliability diagram**: bucket by confidence, plot mean confidence against actual accuracy; the diagonal is perfect calibration, below it is overconfidence.
- **ECE**: the weighted mean of those gaps, one scalar.
- **Brier score**: mean squared error of the probability, punishing both wrongness and miscalibration.

## An implementation trap

In some libraries the field called `confidence` is not a probability at all. Laya's `confidence` is normalized entropy, `1 - H(p)/log(k)` — it measures how peaked the distribution is, not P(correct). Computing ECE from it computes a meaningless quantity; calibration must use the top-1 probability. This book really stepped on it.

**Exercise**: open your library and check how its 'confidence' field is actually computed. If it is entropy, a margin, or anything else, you cannot route on it as a probability directly.
