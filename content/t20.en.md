## Capacity becomes arithmetic

Capacity planning here is far simpler than for an LLM, because there is no KV cache, memory is constant, and latency depends only on input length and batch.

@fig t20-capacity

- **Single-request latency**: measured here at a 401 ms median for one 18-option question on CPU with the multilingual checkpoint, against the vendor's 32.8-to-39.5 ms per question on GPU.
- **Batching gain**: ten questions in one forward pass take the vendor's number from 32.8 ms to 7.2 ms per question — noting this batch means several questions over one state, different from cross-request dynamic batching, which you assemble in your own service layer.
- **Threads**: on CPU, `LAYA_THREADS` above the physical core count slows things through contention.
- **Queueing**: given arrival rate, service time and concurrency, M/M/c gives the queueing delay, which added to service time gives end-to-end P95 and therefore the worker count.

## Size on P95

The bench is that queueing model: drag QPS, per-request latency, worker count and batch size, and watch P95 take off at a particular load — that point is your capacity wall, and it arrives earlier than intuition suggests.

Size on P95 rather than the mean, because a decision service usually hangs inside a synchronous chain: a pretty mean with a blown P95 means one user in twenty waits. In practice add workers until P95 fits **and** utilisation ρ is below 0.85 — the knee of the curve.

**Exercise**: run the queueing model with your real QPS and measured per-request latency, and compute the workers needed to fit P95 into your budget. You will probably need more workers than intuition suggested.
