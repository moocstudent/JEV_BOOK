## The branches

Five questions:

1. How many labels (past twenty, the encoder camp is out).
2. Which language (non-Latin rules out the English checkpoint and a set of English-only projects).
3. Whether you have labelled data (a few hundred rows argue for fine-tuning a classifier first).
4. Whether you already run an LLM (if so, try logit scoring first, near-zero marginal cost).
5. Latency budget and call volume (whether another service is worth keeping alive).

A leaf gives one concrete recommendation rather than a shortlist.

## Shelf life

This book was written on 23 September 2026, eight days after Jev launched and less than a week after the first open implementations appeared. So every specific number — version strings, parameter counts, latencies, benchmark scores, which issues remain open — has a short shelf life.

What does not expire is the method: three baselines, four tables, the threshold-coverage-precision curve, and the rule that every number is re-measured on your own data. These still hold in six months, when the specific leaderboard has probably turned over a few times.

## A re-measurement checklist

Rerun it in six months: check whether the model updated, re-measure accuracy and calibration on your own data, and compare against the numbers you recorded today. Trust the delta, not any README.
