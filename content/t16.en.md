## Accuracy without a baseline has no units

Someone tells you the model is 65% accurate. That sentence carries no information. If it is a balanced binary task, 0.65 is barely better than a coin; if it is an 18-class task, 0.65 is strong; and if your majority class already holds 62%, then 0.65 has done nothing.

@fig t16-base

## Three baselines

- The **random baseline** is `1/k` and tells you where the floor is.
- The **majority-class baseline** is the largest class's share — most often forgotten and most likely to humiliate a model, rising as imbalance grows.
- The **keyword-rule baseline** is the third and most valuable: twenty minutes of `if`-statements and regexes clears 0.5 on many real tasks, at zero latency, zero cost and total interpretability. A model that cannot beat it should end the project.

## The book's measured example

0.505 against a random 0.056 and majority 0.120 — the model did learn, at nine times random. But on a different task whose majority is already 0.62, the same 0.65 has done nothing.

## The minimum format of a report

An accuracy must always appear beside all three baselines, and one that appears alone should be sent back. This is not formalism — it is the only way to turn '0.505' from 'no idea if that's good' into 'nine times random, but not enough to automate'.

**Exercise**: add three baselines to any 'model accuracy X' report you have. You may find a much-praised number is only two points over the majority class.
