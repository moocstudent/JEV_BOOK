## Convert calibration into money

This is the book's most practical chapter. The business does not care about ECE; it cares how much work this removes and how reliable the removed part is. Those two numbers are **coverage** and **precision**, and the threshold connects them.

@fig t9-gate

The method is simple: sweep confidence from low to high and at each threshold compute two numbers — the share of rows above it (coverage, your automation rate) and the accuracy of that share (precision). That curve is what you take into the room: 'at 0.9 we automate 29% and that 29% is 65% correct' carries far more than '50.5% accurate'.

## A test that runs deeper than accuracy

If moving the threshold from 0.5 to 0.95 cuts coverage by two thirds and buys six points of precision, then confidence barely separates right from wrong on this data, the gating design has failed — and gating was the entire value proposition.

This book's run was exactly that: at 0.50, coverage 56.5% and precision 0.623; at 0.95, coverage 20.8% and precision 0.689. Two thirds of coverage gone for 6.6 points of precision.

## Turn it into one number with AUROC

'Can confidence separate right from wrong' becomes a scalar via AUROC: 0.5 is no separation, 0.77 is what Laya reports for `confidence` on its own data, and `act_probability` manages 0.30 — below 0.5, meaning it runs backwards, so do not gate on it.

**Exercise**: plot the threshold-coverage-precision table on your data, add a cost model (what automation saves, what a missed error costs), and find the threshold of maximum net gain. If no threshold makes net gain positive, this model cannot ship here.
