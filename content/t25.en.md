## Two problems behind one framing

You will be asked this: how does a model like Jev differ from ChatGPT, and why is it so much faster? The easy wrong answer is 'Jev is a smaller model'. It is smaller, but that is not the point. The point is that they **solve different problems**.

ChatGPT models 'given the context, what is the next token' and produces language; Jev models 'given a state and some typed questions, what is the distribution of each answer' and produces calibrated decisions. TypeSafe frames this as **System 2** (slow, deliberate, generative reasoning) versus **System 1** (fast, reflexive judgement) — borrowing Kahneman's two systems. One can write, reason, and do anything expressible as text; the other can only answer questions whose **answer space you defined in advance**, but answers them fast and steadily.

## Four axes

**Algorithm**. ChatGPT uses autoregressive decoding: to emit N tokens it runs N forward passes, each conditioned on every previous token and reading a growing KV cache. Jev uses a single forward pass: run the input through once, read logits off a classification head — no sampling loop, no cache growing with the sequence, and the answer is not *generated* but *read*.

**Architecture**. ChatGPT is a decoder-only Transformer, causal (one-way) attention, hundreds of billions of parameters. Jev (and the Laya and Von reimplementations of it) is an encoder-style bidirectional Transformer of hundreds of millions — bidirectional is fine, because you are not emitting left to right. The options are encoded as text spliced into the input (which is why more labels compete for the token budget, see LY2).

**Training objective**. This is the deepest divide. ChatGPT optimises next-token likelihood, then RLHF. Jev optimises a **strictly proper scoring rule** — calibrated probability directly as the target (TypeSafe calls it RLCD but never published it reproducibly; the open clones approximate it with cross-entropy plus Brier plus temperature scaling). The by-product of the first is fluent text; the by-product of the second is a probability you can threshold on.

**Serving stack**. The ChatGPT side is vLLM / TensorRT-LLM, with a paged KV cache, continuous batching, speculative decoding, tensor parallelism across GPUs — a whole apparatus built to make autoregression bearable. The Jev side has no autoregression to optimise, so the stack collapses to a FastAPI process with constant memory that runs on CPU, and batching is simply several questions in one forward pass.

## Why faster: three multiplicative effects

1. **One forward pass against N.** The big one. Emitting a decision as JSON might take thirty to a hundred tokens, and each token is a **separate** forward pass over the whole network. The encoder runs the input once and reads the answer; the answer has no length, so there is nothing to iterate.
2. **A model 100–1000× smaller.** Four hundred million parameters against hundreds of billions; each forward pass is itself far cheaper.
3. **No autoregressive machinery.** No sampling loop, no KV cache growing with the sequence, no speculative-decoding scaffolding to claw back speed. Memory is constant, so even concurrency planning becomes arithmetic (see OP2).

Multiply the three and you get TypeSafe's '193× faster, 445× cheaper' headline, and Laya's more conservative measured ~7×. On the bench, drag 'tokens the LLM must emit' and 'LLM size' and watch the total speed-up be the product of those two.

## The honest boundary

Two things must be said plainly. First, Jev's internals were **never published reproducibly**; the architecture in the table above is what the open alternatives independently **re-derived**, not what was taken from Jev — so do not treat it as confirmation of Jev. Second, this is **not a better ChatGPT**. It cannot write, summarise, or reason open-endedly. You use it as a fast, calibrated **decision head** beside or in front of an LLM (a prompt-injection gate, small-vs-large model routing), not as a replacement for one. Pick the wrong use and no amount of speed helps.
